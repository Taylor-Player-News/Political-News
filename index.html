War powers · Iran

The House is debating a third Iran withdrawal directive today. It has already adopted two, and the forces are still there.

H.Con.Res. 93 is on the floor today. Introduced April 28 by Rep. Seth Moulton and referred to Foreign Affairs, its operative text directs the President, under section 5(c) of the War Powers Resolution (50 U.S.C. 1544(c)), to remove United States Armed Forces from hostilities against Iran, excepting only those elements necessary to defend the United States or an ally from imminent attack, and then only if the President complies fully with section 5(b).

The reason to read the tallies rather than the rhetoric is that Congress has already given this instruction, in identical terms, twice. H.Con.Res. 86 cleared the House on June 3 and the Senate on June 23, which makes it a concurrent resolution adopted by both chambers. H.Con.Res. 89 passed the House again on July 23. Neither carries the force of law — a concurrent resolution is not presented to the President — and the deployment has continued through both.

As of publication the House has not yet convened — it is set to come in at 10:00 a.m. Eastern — and no recorded vote on H.Con.Res. 93 appears in the Clerk’s record. The most recent roll call posted is number 297, taken the evening of September 14. If a vote happens today it will be numbered 298 or higher, and this page will be behind it until tomorrow.

War powers · What the debate adds

The case being made on the floor today, and whose numbers those are

The ranking member of the House Foreign Affairs Committee, Rep. Gregory Meeks, released floor remarks as prepared for today’s debate. He puts the war at seven months against an administration estimate of four to six weeks, and attaches a household price: more than $100 billion in additional gasoline and diesel costs, roughly $800 per household, with gasoline averaging more than $4 a gallon and diesel near $6.

Those are a committee Democrat’s figures in a press release, not an agency series, and they are not sourced in the document itself. Treat them as an argument being advanced until you can match them against Energy Information Administration price data. The procedural claims in the same remarks — that H.Con.Res. 86 passed both chambers and that the House passed H.Con.Res. 89 in July — do check out against the Clerk and Senate records above.

Elections · Courts

The Postal Service will not be enforcing its mail-ballot rule in November

Yesterday the Supreme Court denied the government’s stay application in Postal Service v. California, No. 26A305, leaving a district court’s preliminary injunction in place. The order is two sentences of reasoning: the Government “is unlikely to succeed on the merits of its challenge to the District Court’s preliminary injunction,” and the equitable factors “do not favor a stay.” This is a merits-flavored denial, which is unusual on the emergency docket and worth noting precisely because so many recent orders in this area turned on jurisdiction instead.

What the rule would have required is concrete. Published August 26 as Ballot Mail for Federal Elections, 91 Fed. Reg. 54966, it directs that federal ballot mail carry an election-mail logo, be scannable on high-speed equipment, and bear a barcode unique to each voter; that States submit envelope designs to the Postal Service for review; and that States upload voter information to a Postal Service portal. Non-compliant mail “will not be accepted and will be returned to the” State.

Justice Kavanaugh concurred on narrow grounds: there is “at least a fair prospect” the rule is within the Postal Service’s authority under 39 U.S.C. §401(2), but applying it to the 2026 elections would be arbitrary and capricious because officials lack time to implement it. Justice Alito, joined by Justice Thomas, dissented, arguing some plaintiffs lack standing and the rest press a claim the Court has called a “Hail Mary pass.” Nobody in the case has yet decided whether the rule is lawful.

Immigration · Rulemaking

Student and exchange visas stopped being open-ended today, and nothing was filed to stop it

The Department of Homeland Security rule this app flagged yesterday took effect today. Admission in the F, J and I nonimmigrant classifications changes from “duration of status” to a fixed time period, with a new extension-of-stay procedure, amending 8 CFR parts 214, 248 and 274a. It was published July 17 at 91 Fed. Reg. 44976.

Yesterday this was an open question here, because DHS had written a contingency into its own rule: if the effective date changed at the conclusion of congressional review, it would publish a further notice setting a new date or terminating the rule. A search of the Federal Register for RIN 1653–AA95 returns only the 2025 proposed rule, the July final rule, and a routine Unified Agenda entry. No superseding document was published, so the September 15 date held by default rather than by anyone affirming it.

Safety net · Rulemaking

Eighteen states had SNAP soda-and-candy restrictions posted for comment on the same morning, most of them already running

Today’s Federal Register carries eighteen separate notices from the Food and Nutrition Administration, one per state, inviting comment on demonstration projects that narrow what SNAP benefits can buy. Every one is authorized under section 17(b) of the Food and Nutrition Act of 2008, 7 U.S.C. 2026(b), and every one works the same way: the state asked to amend the definition of food eligible for purchase, and USDA approved it.

The exclusions are not uniform. Hawaii, Indiana and Utah cover only “soft drinks.” Ohio and Virginia cover sweetened beverages. Arkansas reaches “fruit and vegetable drinks with less than 50% natural juice” and “other unhealthy drinks.” Florida, Missouri and Montana add prepared desserts. Approvals came in clusters — June 10 and August 4, 2025, then December 10, 2025 and March 4, 2026 — and requested start dates run from January 1, 2026 all the way out to February 1, 2028.

Read the sequence, because it is the story: comments are due October 15, 2026, and the notices state that public comment is “not required by statute.” Several projects, South Carolina’s among them, have been in operation for weeks. FNA says it is “particularly interested in comments regarding the state’s implementation and operation” — that is, comment on a program already live, not on whether to start one.

Civil service · Rulemaking

OPM reopened a federal-firing rulemaking specifically to take comment on an advocacy group’s report

The comment period on Promoting Employee Accountability closed August 3. Today OPM reopened it for two weeks, to September 29, and said why: it is releasing current OPM data relevant to the proposal, and it “is also interested in receiving public comment on a report published by We the Doers published on August 27, 2026.” The reopening is scoped to the proposal “as informed by the newly released data and the report.”

What is being reopened matters more than the two weeks. The underlying proposal, issued jointly by OPM and the Merit Systems Protection Board on July 2 at 91 Fed. Reg. 40444, would rewrite the procedures for performance-based demotions and removals, non-disciplinary separations and adverse actions across 5 CFR parts 412, 432, 715 and 752 — and would have the Board review penalties under a totality-of-the-circumstances test rather than a rigid application of prescribed factors. An agency inviting comment on a named outside organization’s report is worth watching on its own terms, whatever the report says.

The party breakdowns are the part worth carrying. On the June 4 vote the Democratic caucus split against itself, 91 yes to 117 no, with two voting present. Averaged into a single number, none of that is visible; that is why it is printed as two roll calls and not as a trend.

War powers · Iran

Does a third directive do anything the first two did not?

Both chambers adopted H.Con.Res. 86 in June and the House adopted H.Con.Res. 89 in July. The forces are still in hostilities. Nothing in the record identifies a mechanism by which a third concurrent resolution, which is not presented to the President, compels a different outcome.

What would resolve it: a roll call on H.Con.Res. 93 in the Clerk’s record, numbered 298 or higher — and separately, a section 5(b) report or a withdrawal order. The vote and the withdrawal are different events, and only the second one ends the question.

Elections · Mail ballots

Is the Postal Service ballot-mail rule lawful?

Yesterday’s order decided that the government is unlikely to win and that equity does not favor emergency relief. It did not decide whether the rule is within the Postal Service’s authority — Justice Kavanaugh wrote separately that it may well be, while objecting to its timing.

What would resolve it: a merits ruling on the preliminary injunction in the District of Massachusetts case or on appeal in the First Circuit. Nothing before then settles the rule’s validity for elections after 2026.

Which map does Missouri use?

Mostly answered, and worth recording as such

Yesterday this sat here unresolved. The picture is now clearer: the Missouri Supreme Court held unanimously on September 3 in von Glahn v. Hoskins, No. SC101805, that the referendum petition was legal, sufficient and timely, that the 2025 HB 1 map was never in effect, and that it cannot take effect unless approved by voters in November. The U.S. Supreme Court’s September 10 stay of a conflicting federal district court order left that state ruling operative.

Reporting adds the operational consequence, which is not in either order: the Secretary of State has certified and directed local election authorities to use the 2022 districts, and the 2025 map goes to voters as Proposition A on November 3.

What would still resolve it fully: the Eighth Circuit’s disposition of the appeal, or a certiorari ruling — the September 10 stay dissolves automatically if certiorari is denied.
